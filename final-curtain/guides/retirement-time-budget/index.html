Retirement Has a Time Budget Too

October 30, 2026

An ordinary Tuesday can make a retirement plan more concrete. What would you want the morning to contain? Which responsibilities would still be there? Who would you spend time with, and what would you leave open?

Writing down one realistic week gives those questions somewhere to land. It also helps distinguish the hours released by work from the many activities that continue after it.

By IDEASpike, the developer of Final Curtain.

Start With the Week You Actually Expect

A week contains 168 hours before and after retirement. The allocation changes. Sleep, meals, household work, care and appointments still need space.

Begin with a current-week list. Then copy it and change the work and commute entries to match the arrangement you are exploring. Someone moving to part-time work needs a different version from someone ending paid work entirely.

Keep other entries visible while you do this. It is easy to imagine the empty space left by work and overlook responsibilities that were previously squeezed around it.

Give an Ordinary Week Some Shape

Here is a fictional example of a week without paid work. These values are chosen inputs for illustration, not recommendations about sleep, care or activity levels.

ActivityEntered hours each week
Sleep56
Meals and household work21
Family and caring responsibilities14
Movement and time outdoors7
Friends and community7
Learning or creative work10
Errands and administration5
Unallocated and flexible time48
Total168

The flexible portion can include rest, changing plans and interests the person has not chosen yet. There is no requirement to fill it all with named activities.

Your list may look very different. Caring for another person, maintaining a home, travelling or working occasionally can change the available capacity substantially. Enter the commitments you expect to have, then revisit them as you learn.

Try the Ordinary Tuesday Test

Take one of the week’s ordinary days and place a few activities in it. For example, a fictional Tuesday could contain eight hours of sleep, three hours of meals and household work, a one-hour walk, a two-hour class, one hour with a friend and one hour of administration. That leaves eight hours flexible within the 24-hour day.

That is one day, rather than a template to repeat seven times. Check it against the weekly totals and consider the timing: a class might run on one afternoon, a friend might be working during the day, and appointments can require travel.

The point is to discover what makes the imagined week workable. You may want fewer scheduled activities, a recurring social anchor, a larger buffer or a gradual transition away from work.

Keep Time and Calendar Placement Together

A weekly allocation can show that several interests fit in total. A calendar check shows whether they can fit when they actually occur.

Include travel and preparation when an activity needs them. Avoid counting the same hour twice: if a walk with a friend is one hour, enter it once or deliberately use a combined category. A tidy-looking total becomes misleading when overlapping activities are each counted in full.

Leave variable responsibilities visible. “Appointments and administration vary” is useful information; a precise-looking number should not hide the uncertainty.

Compare a Transition You Care About

Choose one comparison: full-time work and part-time work, one retirement date and another, or two different allocations after work. Keep the common assumptions the same at first.

Then describe the difference in ordinary terms. Perhaps the alternative permits a weekday course, more daylight outdoors, a regular family visit or a quieter pace around existing responsibilities.

A time model can make these possibilities easier to discuss. Whether retirement is affordable belongs in a separate financial assessment. This worksheet does not estimate investment returns, pension income or the resources you need to retire.

Use the weekly time worksheet to keep your current and alternative versions. Returning to it occasionally lets you adjust the plan without turning every day into something to optimize.
