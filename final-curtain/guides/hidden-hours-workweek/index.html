The Hidden Hours in a 40-Hour Workweek

October 30, 2026

An eight-hour workday can occupy more than eight hours of your day. Getting to work, getting home and preparing for the day also need a place in the week.

Before comparing a new job, a shorter commute or fewer work hours, it helps to count that surrounding time. The calculation is small. The distinction can change which option you want to explore.

By IDEASpike, the developer of Final Curtain.

Count the Whole Working Week

Start with three separate entries: paid work, commuting and preparation that exists because of work. Use your own typical week rather than an unusually quiet or difficult one.

Here is a fictional example:

Work-related activityCalculationHours each week
Paid work8 hours × 5 days40
Travel to and from work1 hour total each day × 5 days5
Work-specific preparation30 minutes × 5 days2.5
Total40 + 5 + 2.547.5

In this example, the work arrangement occupies 47.5 hours of the week. The 7.5 hours around the job are real time even though they are absent from the paid-hours figure.

The table needs a clear boundary. Count the round-trip commute once. Include only the extra preparation attributable to work; ordinary eating, washing and dressing still happen on a day off. If you regularly answer messages outside your contracted hours, give that time its own entry rather than assuming it fits inside the forty.

Put That Number Inside a Week

A week contains 168 hours. Suppose the person in this illustration allocates eight hours to sleep each night: 56 hours a week. That is an example input, not a sleep recommendation.

Their remaining calculation is:

168 − 56 − 47.5 = 64.5 hours.

Those 64.5 hours still contain meals, household work, caring responsibilities, appointments and everything else. They are the hours left before those activities are entered. Calling the whole amount “free time” would hide the very commitments the exercise is meant to reveal.

Add the remaining categories before choosing what you can change. A simple list is enough: food and household work, family and care, movement, administration, interests, and unallocated time.

Give each hour one place in the total. A walk with a friend can belong to a combined activity or one primary category. Adding the full hour to both exercise and social time makes the week appear to contain an extra hour.

Compare One Change

Now suppose the same person could reduce their round-trip commute from one hour a day to thirty minutes. Hold work and preparation constant:

ActivityCurrent weekShorter commute
Paid work4040
Commute52.5
Work-specific preparation2.52.5
Total work-related hours47.545

The difference is 2.5 hours per working week. Across an illustrative 48 working weeks, that is 120 hours. Use your actual number of commuting weeks for your own calculation.

This describes the time difference between the two assumptions. Whether either arrangement is available, affordable or desirable needs its own consideration.

Make the difference concrete by naming a use for it. Two and a half hours might hold a class and its travel time, one longer visit, or several quieter evenings. Check the calendar as well as the total: five half-hour gaps have a different shape from one uninterrupted block.

A Ten-Minute Exercise

Write down your current work hours and the time around them. Add sleep and your other recurring commitments until the entries fit inside 168 hours. Leave a visible allowance for variable days.

Copy the list and change one assumption. Keep everything else constant for the first comparison. Then answer three questions:

  1. How many hours change in an ordinary working week?
  2. Where would those hours occur during the day?
  3. What would you choose to give them to?

The weekly time worksheet provides a blank version of this exercise. You can keep the comparison on paper or in a spreadsheet and revise it as your circumstances change.
